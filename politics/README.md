## Problem

Politics often attract people that want power. Once in office, politicians spend significant effort on remaining in power.

Corruption is also a wide-spread occurrence. In Germany it regularly occurs that politicians get well-paid jobs at companies, during or after their term.
In other countries it can take even more extreme forms.

This whole framework of having elections every four years does not
help encourage long-term thinking in politics.
Sometimes expert committees are assembled and their opinions ignored.
How could a decision be made that hurts today, but will be beneficial long-term. After 20, 100 or even 1000 years?

Direct democracy (e.g. Switzerland) or absolute monarchy (e.g. Saudi Arabia and Oman) have some advantages but also their own set of problems.

[demographics](../demographics/) means, the average voter is age 50+ and this is the more wealthy, more connected half of the population, their perspectives are often disproportionately considered in politics. pensions are raised but young people don't get the support they need.

Debate now runs on a media system that rewards outrage over accuracy, and evidence can be fabricated cheaply. See [information](../information/).

The hardest part is not knowing what to do. It is acting when the cost is now, the benefit is later, and everyone else can wait. See [coordination](../coordination/).

## Research

**How much democracy there is.** [V-Dem's Democracy Report 2026](https://www.v-dem.net/documents/75/V-Dem_Institute_Democracy_Report_2026_lowres.pdf), scoring 2025, counts 92 autocracies against 87 democracies. 74% of the world population (about 6 billion) lives under autocracy, 7% (0.6 billion) under liberal democracy. Weighted by population, the world's level of liberal democracy is back to its 1978 value: the gains of the "third wave" that began in 1974 are nearly erased. 44 countries are autocratising, 18 democratising. The share of people in an autocratising country went from 9% in 2005 to 41% (3.4 billion) in 2025; the share in a democratising one fell from 11% to 5%. Freedom of expression declined in 44 countries in 2025, against 7 in 2005.

[Freedom House](https://freedomhouse.org/article/new-report-global-freedom-declined-20th-consecutive-year-2025) records 2025 as the twentieth consecutive year of net decline: 54 countries down, 35 up, and 21% of the world's people in countries rated Free, against 46% two decades earlier.

**Where the data is weak.** Both indices rest on expert coders. [Little and Meng (2024)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/measuring-democratic-backsliding/9EE2044CDA598BD815349912E61189D8) show that objective indicators — incumbent vote shares, turnover, term-limit evasion — barely move over the past decade, and argue expert scores drift as coders' standards shift. The [reply in the same issue](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ps-political-science-and-politics/article/conceptual-and-measurement-issues-in-assessing-democratic-backsliding/7A620BD91885C932B48E6783BC32CA24) answers that no measure of democracy is judgment-free, that objective proxies carry their own biases, and that the balance of evidence still shows backsliding. The dispute is over magnitude, not direction.

**Trust.** The [OECD Trust Survey](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2026-results_9eb63fec-en.html), third wave, fielded in 2025 across 33 OECD countries, finds trust in national government stable at about 40%, with 43% reporting low or no trust. The [2023 wave](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/oecd-survey-on-drivers-of-trust-in-public-institutions-2024-results_9a20554b-en/full-report/executive-summary_7c5c9309.html) (about 60,000 respondents, 30 countries) had 39% against 44%, two points below 2021. Trust in the police (63%), courts (54%) and civil service (45%) runs well above that: the deficit is specific to elected politics, not to the state.

**Spending timed to elections.** [Shi and Svensson](https://www.jakobsvensson.com/uploads/9/9/1/0/99107788/pbc_jpubec.pdf) find deficits rise by close to 1% of GDP in election years across a large panel, but [Brender and Drazen](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w10539/w10539.pdf) show the effect is concentrated in new democracies and mostly vanishes in established ones. A [meta-analysis of 1,198 estimates from 88 studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11127-016-0364-1) (Public Choice, 2016) finds an election-year rise in spending and debt that is statistically solid and substantively small.

**Whose preferences win.** [Gilens and Page (2014)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/testing-theories-of-american-politics-elites-interest-groups-and-average-citizens/62327F513959D0A304D4893B382B992B) coded 1,779 US policy questions and found economic elites and business groups have substantial independent influence while average citizens have close to none. It is contested: [Bashir (2015)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/2053168015608896) and later work by Branham, Soroka and Wlezien note that rich and average preferences agree on roughly nine issues in ten, and where they part the rich win only modestly more often. Equivalent European data barely exists.

**Money and access.** US federal lobbying reached [$4.4 billion in 2024](https://www.opensecrets.org/news/2025/02/federal-lobbying-set-new-record-in-2024/) and [about $5 billion in 2025](https://www.opensecrets.org/news/2026/01/lobbying-firms-took-in-a-record-5-billion-in-2025/). The EU transparency register held roughly 14,800 registrants as of 2025; declared spending, reported only in broad brackets, sums to somewhere around €1.6-2.2 billion a year. The digital industry alone declared [€151 million in 2025, up about 56% since 2021](https://corporateeurope.org/en/2025/10/revealed-tech-industry-now-spending-record-eu151-million-lobbying-eu). Germany's [Lobbyregister](https://www.lobbyregister.bundestag.de/suche), mandatory since 1 January 2022, listed 6,958 entries in September 2026. Its cooling-off rule for ministers, in force since 2015, allows a ban of up to 12 months, or 18 in serious cases, decided case by case ([Lobbypedia](https://lobbypedia.de/wiki/Karenzzeit_Deutschland)).

**Deliberation as a fix, and its ceiling.** The [OECD's database of representative deliberative processes](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/innovative-citizen-participation-and-new-democratic-institutions_339306da-en.html) catalogues over 700 cases since 1979, most since 2010. Ireland's [Citizens' Assembly on the Eighth Amendment](https://participedia.net/case/irish-citizens-assembly-the-eighth-amendment) seated 99 randomly selected members under a judge, met over five weekends in 2016-17, heard 25 experts and about 12,000 public submissions, and recommended repeal; the 2018 referendum passed with 66.4%. It worked because parliament had agreed in advance to put the outcome to a vote. France's 2019-20 [climate convention](https://www.conventioncitoyennepourleclimat.fr/) produced 149 proposals promised "without filter"; the government's own tracker counts most as implemented in whole or in part, campaigners count far fewer. The standing objection, argued by Lafont among others, is that a randomly chosen assembly asks everyone else to defer to people they did not pick and cannot remove ([overview](https://delibdemjournal.org/article/id/1501/)). Sortition removes the re-election incentive; it does not supply legitimacy. See [coordination](../coordination/).
