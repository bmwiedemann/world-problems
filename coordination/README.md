## Problem

Most problems in this project share one shape. The cost of acting falls on whoever acts, the benefit is shared by everyone, and the damage of not acting is spread thin and arrives late.

So the rational move for each actor is to wait for the others. Emissions, fish stocks, aquifers, antibiotics and arms races all follow this pattern.

There is no authority above states. Treaties are agreements between parties who each keep the option to defect, and defection is usually cheap.

Decisions are made by people with four-year horizons on problems with hundred-year horizons. Costs now, benefits after the decider is gone. See [politics](../politics/).

Discounting the future is rational for an individual and ruinous for a species.

The general case is that actors follow the rewards they face, not the outcome anyone wants. Coordination problems are the subset where those rewards are set by other actors who cannot be bound. See [incentives](../incentives/).

This is the bottleneck. Nearly every technical solution elsewhere in this project already exists on paper and is not deployed because of this problem.

## Research

**The model.** The standard frame is the n-player public goods game: a contribution costs the contributor in full and returns only a fraction of the benefit, so defection dominates even though everyone does better if all contribute. Common-pool resources add rivalry on top — my catch is not available to you — the shape Garrett Hardin popularised in 1968. Scott Barrett's model of [self-enforcing agreements](https://academic.oup.com/oep/article-abstract/46/Supplement_1/878/2568546) (Oxford Economic Papers, 1994) sharpens the pessimism: without sanctions, treaties are stable only where the coalition is small or the gains from cooperating are small.

**Ostrom's exception.** Elinor Ostrom's *Governing the Commons* (1990) documented local commons that neither privatised nor collapsed, and extracted eight design principles: clear boundaries, rules matched to local conditions, users making the rules, monitoring accountable to users, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, a recognised right to organise, and nested layers. A meta-analysis of 91 case studies by [Cox, Arnold and Villamayor-Tomás (2010)](https://www.ecologyandsociety.org/vol15/iss4/art38/) found the principles well supported empirically, with monitoring and graduated sanctions doing much of the work. The open question is scale: the principles assume users who know each other, can watch each other, and share a legal umbrella. Ostrom's own answer was [polycentric governance](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.100.3.641) — many overlapping units acting at once instead of one global treaty — but that is a research programme, not a demonstrated planetary-scale result.

**Montreal versus Kyoto and Paris.** The Montreal Protocol is the one clear success: ratified by all 197 parties, with [over 99% of controlled ozone-depleting substance production phased out](https://www.unep.org/ozonaction/who-we-are/about-montreal-protocol). The features credited are specific and hard to copy: few producers, chemically close and cheap substitutes, a fund covering developing countries' incremental costs, and [Article 4](https://ozone.unep.org/treaties/montreal-protocol/articles/article-4-control-trade-non-parties), which bans trade in controlled substances with non-parties, so staying outside costs more than joining.

Kyoto had binding targets. On final 2008–2012 data, [Shishlov, Morel and Bellassen (2016)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14693062.2016.1164658) find all 36 fully participating Annex B parties in legal compliance, nine of them only after buying units through the flexibility mechanisms — and Canada withdrew in 2012 rather than face its own target. Paris replaced targets with pledge-and-review: countries set their own contributions, and the compliance committee under [Article 15](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/resource/parisagreement_publication.pdf) must be "non-adversarial and non-punitive". The [UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2025](https://www.unep.org/resources/emissions-gap-report-2025) puts full implementation of pledges at roughly 2.3–2.5 °C of warming this century, and current policies at up to 2.8 °C. See [climate-change](../climate-change/).

**Enforcement proposals.** Nordhaus's [climate clubs](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.15000001) (AER, 2015) formalises the Montreal trick: members price carbon and levy a small uniform tariff on non-members, which in his model yields large stable coalitions. The EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism [entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026](https://trade.ec.europa.eu/access-to-markets/en/news/start-definitive-period-cbam-eu) for iron and steel, cement, aluminium, fertiliser, electricity and hydrogen — the first real test.

**What the future is worth.** The Stern Review (2006) used a pure rate of time preference of 0.1% and an elasticity of 1, giving a consumption discount rate near 1.4% and justifying large spending now ([Commons Library summary](https://researchbriefings.files.parliament.uk/documents/SN04739/SN04739.pdf)). [Nordhaus (2007)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jel.45.3.686) argued the conclusion rests almost entirely on that near-zero rate, and that discounting should follow observed market returns, implying slower action. The disagreement is ethical, not empirical.

**Punishment in the lab.** [Fehr and Gächter (2000)](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.90.4.980) showed contributions in public goods games decay without sanctions and hold up when players can pay to punish free-riders. [Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter (2008)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1153808) ran the same design across 16 participant pools and found punishment often aimed at high contributors instead — "antisocial punishment" — in societies with weaker norms of civic cooperation and rule of law. Sanctions raise cooperation only where norms already back them, a caution for any enforcement scheme laid across unlike states. See [politics](../politics/).
