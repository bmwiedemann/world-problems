## Problem

[AI](../ai/) can do more and more jobs at a faster, more consistent pace and at a lower price than humans, so there will be many jobs lost, causing large unemployment.

Having all robots (including the massive datacenters behind them) owned by few and generating profits only for them, while 90% of the others are unemployable and poor will not work out.

Without income people cannot sustain themselves and not buy products to keep the economy flowing.

Growth has assumed cheap materials and cheap energy. Both are getting more expensive to extract. See [resources](../resources/) and [energy](../energy/).

Prices ignore damage that has no owner: emissions, aquifers, fish stocks, persistent chemicals. Those costs do not disappear, they are just paid by someone else later. See [coordination](../coordination/).

## Research

In the US nonfarm business sector the share of output paid to labour fell from 65.8% in Q1 1947 to a low of 56.0% in Q4 2011, recovering to 58.4% by Q3 2016 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2017/labor-share-of-output-has-declined-since-1947.htm)). Globally the [ILO](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/global-labour-income-share-declines-putting-upward-pressure-inequality-sdg) finds the labour income share fell 0.6 percentage points from 2019 to 2022 and has been flat since; at its 2004 level, workers would have received about US$2.4 trillion more in 2024 alone.

The main mechanical explanation is that machines got cheap. [Karabarbounis and Neiman (2014, QJE)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19136) find the decline runs across most countries and most industries since the early 1980s, and that the falling relative price of investment goods explains roughly half of it.

The dissent is about measurement. [Koh, Santaeulàlia-Llopis and Zheng (2020, Econometrica)](https://www.econometricsociety.org/publications/econometrica/2020/11/01/labor-share-decline-and-intellectual-property-products-capital) argue the entire measured US decline follows from national accounts starting to treat software and R&D as capital rather than expense. If they are right, part of the trend is an accounting change.

From the [World Inequality Report 2022](https://wid.world/news-article/world-inequality-report-2022/), as of 2021:

* top 10% of adults own 76% of global household wealth, bottom 50% own 2%
* top 10% take 52% of global income, bottom 50% take 8%
* the top 1% captured 38% of all new wealth since the mid-1990s, the bottom 50% got 2%

[Acemoglu and Restrepo (2020, JPE)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w23285) use variation in industrial robot adoption across US commuting zones, 1990 to 2007. One extra robot per thousand workers lowers the employment-to-population ratio by about 0.2 percentage points and wages by about 0.42%; locally, one robot displaces roughly 5.6 workers. In [Econometrica (2022)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w28920) they attribute 50-70% of the change in the US wage structure over four decades to falling relative wages of workers specialised in routine tasks in fast-automating industries.

Historically, technology created the jobs it destroyed. [Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller (2024, QJE)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w30389) find most current US employment is in job specialties that did not exist in 1940. But new work was middle-paid production and clerical from 1940 to 1980, and high-paid professional or low-paid service since. The demand-eroding effect of automating innovations intensified over the last four decades while the demand-raising effect of augmenting ones did not. The record is reassuring about totals, not about who gets the new jobs.

Evidence on generative AI is thin and descriptive. From ADP payroll records covering November 2022 to June 2026, [Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/) find no economy-wide displacement, but employment of 22-25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations sits about 19% below where it would be had it tracked less-exposed peers, through reduced hiring rather than layoffs. They call these correlations, not causal estimates. See [ai](../ai/).

Finland paid 2,000 unemployed people €560/month in 2017-2018. Employment effects were small, [+6 days over a year to 78 days on average](https://vatt.fi/en/-/results-of-the-basic-income-experiment-small-employment-effects-better-perceived-economic-security-and-mental-wellbeing), while self-reported wellbeing improved clearly. The [OpenResearch](https://www.openresearchlab.org/findings/nber-working-paper-employment) study gave 3,000 low-income Americans US$1,000/month for three years against 2,000 controls on $50: participation fell 2.0 percentage points, hours fell 1.3-1.4 per week, non-transfer income fell about $1,500/year. Both are transfers to a small group inside a working wage economy. Neither tests what happens when everyone gets the payment and prices and wages adjust.

The [EU Tax Observatory](https://www.taxobservatory.eu/publication/global-tax-evasion-report-2024/) estimates about US$1 trillion of multinational profit was shifted to tax havens in 2022, some 35% of the profits booked outside headquarters countries, costing close to 10% of worldwide corporate tax revenue. The OECD's 15% global minimum tax began applying in 2024, but coverage is partial, carve-outs are broad, and [its expected yield has been revised down repeatedly](https://www.cato.org/commentary/global-minimum-tax-shortfall-undercuts-oecds-own-selling-points). Any plan that pays displaced workers out of the returns to capital has to solve that first. See [politics](../politics/) and [coordination](../coordination/).
