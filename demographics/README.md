## Problem

Most western countries have a fertility-rate below the replacement-rate of 2.1 so population would be shrinking if there were no immigration. Some developing countries are getting there as well.

This causes a shift in demographics where a growing percentage of the population is 50+ years old.
Older people go into retirement, leave the workforce and need support by the society.
Letting fewer and fewer younger people support a growing number of old people is not sustainable.

Immigration can fill the gap in numbers, but the sending regions are the ones under most climate and food pressure, and receiving voters resist. See [migration](../migration/).

More old people also means more care needed from fewer workers. See [health](../health/).

Falling fertility has causes beyond choice, including possible chemical ones. See [pollution](../pollution/).

## Research

### Where fertility stands

The global total fertility rate (TFR) was 2.25 live births per woman in 2024, down from 3.31 in 1990. More than half of all countries are below the replacement level of 2.1, and nearly a fifth are below 1.4 ([UN World Population Prospects 2024](https://population.un.org/wpp/)). Some 2024 values:

* South Korea 0.75, the lowest ever recorded anywhere, up from 0.72 in 2023 ([Statistics Korea](https://kostat.go.kr/board.es?mid=a20108010000&bid=11773))
* Japan 1.15, with 686,061 births, a ninth straight record low ([MHLW data](https://www.nippon.com/en/japan-data/h02429/))
* EU 1.34, down from 1.38 in 2023; Spain 1.10, France 1.61; mean age of mother at first birth 29.9 years ([Eurostat](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20260306-1))
* United States 53.8 births per 1,000 women aged 15-44, down 22% from the 2007 peak ([NCHS](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK617972/))
* Sub-Saharan Africa about 4.3; over one in ten countries worldwide are still above 4

### Momentum

Low fertility does not stop growth for decades. World population was 8.2 billion in 2024 and is projected to peak near 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s. Momentum embedded in today's young age structure supplies 79% of the increase through 2054, about 1.4 billion people: the number of women of reproductive age keeps rising, to roughly 2.2 billion in the late 2050s.

The mechanism runs in reverse too. In the 63 countries whose population had already peaked by 2024 (28% of humanity, including China, Germany, Japan and Russia), women of reproductive age are projected to fall 33% by 2054, and by the late 2030s half the women there will be past childbearing age. Pronatalist policy acts on a shrinking base.

### How far the forecasts have moved

The UN's figure for 2100 is now 6% lower - about 700 million people - than the projection made a decade earlier, mostly because Chinese fertility came in far below expectation. The estimated probability that world population peaks this century rose from roughly 30% to 80% over the same period.

Serious people still disagree. The IHME [Global Burden of Disease 2021 forecast](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(24)00550-6/fulltext) puts global TFR at 1.83 by 2050 and 1.59 by 2100, below the UN, with African fertility falling much faster - Sudan below replacement around 2045, versus after 2095 in the UN model. Nearly all the long-run uncertainty sits in sub-Saharan Africa, where vital registration is thin and estimates lean on periodic surveys.

### The pension arithmetic

Across the OECD, people aged 65+ per 100 aged 20-64 went from 22 in 2000 to 33 in 2025, and is projected to reach 52 by 2050 ([OECD Pensions at a Glance 2025](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/pensions-at-a-glance-2025_e40274c1-en.html)) - roughly 3.0 workers per retiree falling to 1.9.

Pay-as-you-go systems pay out what they collect, so `pension / average wage = contribution rate x workers per retiree`. Hold the contribution rate fixed and pensions fall about 37% relative to wages by 2050; hold pensions fixed relative to wages and contributions must rise about 58%. The only other levers are more workers, later retirement, or general taxation.

The choice already made is visible in the projections: OECD public pension spending rises only from 8.8% of GDP in 2023 to 10.0% in 2050, against a 69% rise in the old-age ratio, because benefit formulas and retirement ages have been legislated down and up respectively. The adjustment is landing on future retirees. The OECD also finds migration can buy time but cannot hold the ratio down without an ever-increasing net inflow - see [migration](../migration/) and [economics](../economics/).

### Causes, and what policy can do

Stated reasons and measured causes do not line up. In a UNFPA survey across 14 countries, 39% named financial limits as a barrier to their desired family size, and about one in five cited worries about [climate change](../climate-change/), conflict and pandemics ([State of World Population 2025](https://www.unfpa.org/swp2025)). When economists look for the mechanism, it is hard to find: Kearney, Levine and Pardue traced the US decline from 2007 to 2020 across every group of women and could identify no economic, policy or social factor beyond the Great Recession that explains much of it, attributing the rest to shifting priorities across cohorts ([JEP 2022](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fjep.36.1.151)).

Housing has real but modest effects: a 10% rise in real house prices is associated with 0.01-0.03 fewer births per woman, and the sign flips between owners, who gain wealth, and renters ([Dettling and Kearney, NBER 2011](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17485)).

Pronatalist transfers work weakly. Estimated cash-transfer effects cluster around +0.08 to +0.35 on TFR, much of it tempo - births brought forward rather than added ([FREE Network review](https://freepolicybriefs.org/2020/01/15/childbearing-pronatalist-policies/)). South Korea has run large family-support programmes for two decades and still has the world's lowest rate. No high-income country has returned to 2.1 through policy, so planning that assumes recovery is planning on the least-supported outcome.
