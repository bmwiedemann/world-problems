## Problem

Most bad outcomes are produced by people following their incentives correctly. Stupidity and malice explain a little. The reward structure explains more, and unlike the other two it can be changed.

The same shape appears at every scale:

* A manager meets the quarterly number by deferring maintenance. The failure arrives after the promotion.
* A company sells what is profitable rather than what is good, because profit is what it is judged on. See [economics](../economics/).
* A minister starts a programme that pays out before the next election and bills after it. See [politics](../politics/).
* A platform optimises engagement, because engagement is the thing it can measure and sell. See [information](../information/).
* Nobody develops new antibiotics, because the correct use of a new antibiotic is to not use it. See [health](../health/).

Once a measure becomes a target it stops measuring what it did. People optimise the number, and the number comes apart from the thing it stood for.

Incentives beat intentions. Ethics codes, mission statements and appeals to responsibility lose to a payment scheme that rewards the opposite, and they lose quietly, without anyone deciding to do wrong.

Costs that land on nobody in particular are the common case: later, elsewhere, or spread thin across everyone. Nothing in the reward structure registers them, so they accumulate unopposed.

The obvious fix is harder than it looks. Every incentive is gamed along whatever dimension was left unmeasured, and a bounty on dead cobras produces cobra farms. Designing incentives is itself a design problem, and a slow one to correct.

Where the incentive failure runs between actors who cannot bind each other, it becomes a [coordination](../coordination/) problem. That is the hardest version, but not the only one.

## Research

**The model and its failure mode.** Principal-agent theory starts from a principal who cannot observe effort and so pays on a measurable proxy. [Holmström and Milgrom (1991)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jleo/7.special_issue.24) supplied the part that does the damage: when an agent has several tasks and only some of them are measurable, sharpening the incentive on the measured task pulls effort off the unmeasured ones. Where the important dimensions cannot be measured, the best incentive is a weak one, or a flat salary.

**Two laws.** [Campbell (1979)](https://doi.org/10.1016/0149-7189%2879%2990048-X) put it as a rule: the more a quantitative indicator is used for social decision-making, the more it will be corrupted, and the more it will distort the process it was meant to monitor. Goodhart's 1975 remark about monetary aggregates is the same point from central banking.

**What the gaming evidence shows.** [Jacob and Levitt (QJE 2003)](https://www.nber.org/papers/w9413) combined unexpected score jumps with suspicious answer patterns to estimate serious teacher or administrator cheating in at least 4-5% of Chicago elementary classrooms per year, rising sharply with small changes in the stakes. [Bevan and Hood (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9299.2006.00600.x) catalogued gaming under England's NHS regime of "targets and terror" and drew the parallel to Soviet output quotas.

The honest counterweight: the targets often also worked. [Propper and co-authors (2008)](https://doi.org/10.2202/1935-1682.1863) compared England with Scotland, which set no targets, and found England's elective waiting lists genuinely shortened; [Kelman and Friedman (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/mun028) found little distortion around the four-hour emergency target. Where a target did override everything else the cost was concrete: the [Francis Inquiry](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/report-of-the-mid-staffordshire-nhs-foundation-trust-public-inquiry) (2013) found the failures at Mid Staffordshire were "in part the consequence of allowing a focus on reaching national access targets, achieving financial balance and seeking foundation trust status to be at the cost of delivering acceptable standards of care". See [health](../health/).

**A quota that manufactured fraud.** The [CFPB's 2016 consent order](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/092016_cfpb_WFBconsentorder.pdf) against Wells Fargo is the best-documented case. From the bank's own analysis of May 2011 to July 2015: 1,534,280 deposit accounts opened that may not have been authorised, some funded by moving customers' money without consent, and 565,443 credit-card applications made using customers' details. Roughly 5,300 employees were fired; the penalty was $100 million. The [SEC's 2020 settlement](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2020-38) extended the period to 2002-2016 and the total to $3 billion. Nobody set out to commit fraud. They set a cross-selling target.

**Paying for a behaviour can stop it.** [Gneezy and Rustichini (2000)](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/ucpjlstud/v_3a29_3ay_3a2000_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a1-17.htm) fined parents who collected children late from Israeli day-care centres. Lateness rose, and stayed high after the fine was withdrawn: the fine had repriced a social obligation as a service you could buy. [Mellström and Johannesson (2008)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/tpr/jeurec/v6y2008i4p845-863.html) offered SEK 50 for blood donation and women's donation rates fell by almost half; letting donors pass the money to charity restored them. [Deci, Koestner and Ryan's meta-analysis of 128 experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627) is the general result.

**Executive pay.** [Bertrand and Mullainathan (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1162/00335530152466269) found CEO pay "responds about as much to a 'lucky' dollar as to a general dollar" — it tracks sector-wide moves the CEO did not cause — and less so where a large shareholder is watching. [Frydman and Jenter](https://www.nber.org/papers/w16585) conclude that neither optimal contracting nor managerial power fits the record alone. What equity incentives reliably buy is reporting behaviour: [Burns and Kedia (2006)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfineco.2004.12.003) find the sensitivity of a CEO's option holdings to the share price predicts misreporting. In a survey of 401 financial executives, [Graham, Harvey and Rajgopal](https://www.nber.org/papers/w10550) found 78% would give up economic value for smoother earnings and 55% would drop a clearly positive-value project rather than miss the quarter's consensus. See [economics](../economics/).

**The cobra, honestly.** The Delhi cobra bounty is repeated everywhere without a source; treat it as a parable. The Hanoi version is documented. [Michael Vann](https://press-files.anu.edu.au/downloads/press/n7524/pdf/great_hanoi.pdf), who read the 1902 files, records over 20,000 rats killed on the worst day, then a collapse to none — residents were farming rats, cutting off the tails for the bounty, releasing them to breed, and smuggling more in from across Tonkin.

## Solutions

Inside one firm or one jurisdiction somebody can change the rules by decision rather than negotiation, so the evidence here is far better than in [coordination](../coordination/). It is also more damning: the mechanisms that work are known and mostly not used. The same tests apply — the rule must move money, and the number that triggers it must come from somebody other than the party it judges — plus one more at this scale. The instrument survives only if the payer can see where the money went.

### Price the damage, and expect to lose the argument

Direct carbon pricing is the best-measured intervention in this project. As of the World Bank's [2026 review](https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/handle/10986/44787), 87 instruments cover 29% of global emissions and raised over US$107 billion in 2025, at an average price near US$21/tCO2e. A [meta-analysis of 80 ex-post evaluations across 21 schemes](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-48512-w) finds reductions of 5-21%, or 4-15% after correcting for publication bias; [Green (2021)](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1748-9326/abdae9) puts them at 0-2% a year. It works, but nowhere near the scale of the problem, because the prices are not at that scale either.

It also keeps losing. Australia repealed its carbon price in 2014, and British Columbia's — the most studied in the world — was [eliminated on 1 April 2025](https://www2.gov.bc.ca/gov/content/taxes/sales-taxes/motor-fuel-carbon-tax/carbon-tax-elimination). Global carbon tax revenue fell about 20% in 2025, mostly because Canada's fuel charge went to zero. See [climate change](../climate-change/).

Deposit-refund, by contrast, nobody repeals. Germany's one-way deposit has run since 2003, with returns reported near 98% by operators and [above 96% by the federal government](https://dserver.bundestag.de/btd/19/043/1904359.pdf). The difference is the shape, not the strength: the payer gets the money back by complying, so there is no constituency of losers.

**Cap-and-trade, honestly.** US power-sector SO2 fell from [15.7 million tons in 1990 to 650,000 in 2023](https://www.epa.gov/power-sector/progress-report-emissions-reductions). [Schmalensee and Stavins](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18306) put the saving over prescriptive regulation at 15% to perhaps 90%, but attribute about a third of the early reductions to rail deregulation that made low-sulphur coal cheap. The market then died of litigation: when the D.C. Circuit vacated CAIR in July 2008 the allowance price fell from $315 to $115 in a day, and reached $0.56 by 2012.

### Make the decider hold the money before the damage

**Escrow works when the cash moves first.** German utilities paid [€24.1 billion into KENFO in July 2017](https://www.kenfo.de/ueber-den-kenfo) and the state assumed the waste-disposal liability. The fund held €25.6 billion at the end of 2025 after paying out €5.3 billion. The money moved while the payers still existed.

**Bonds that are not bonds do not.** US coal is the only extractive sector where federal rules let a company pledge its own balance sheet instead of posting security. About $3.86 billion of self-bonded reclamation was outstanding in 2016, $2.4 billion of it held by firms then in bankruptcy, and [GAO](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-18-305) records the gap still open in February 2026.

**Late liability prices nothing.** Manufacturers knew PFOA was ["highly toxic when inhaled and moderately toxic when ingested" by 1970](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10237242/); the penalty for not reporting it was $16.45 million in 2005, and [3M's water-utility settlement](https://news.3m.com/2024-04-01-3M-Settlement-with-Public-Water-Suppliers-to-Address-PFAS-in-Drinking-Water-Receives-Final-Court-Approval) runs to $12.5 billion, paid 2024-2036. A signal fifty years late is not an incentive. See [pollution](../pollution/).

### Decide who writes the number

The strongest anti-gaming device found anywhere is not a penalty. Denmark audited [42,784 tax filers at random](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~saez/kleven-knudsen-kreiner-pedersen-saezEMA11taxaudit.pdf): evasion was 0.23% on third-party-reported income against 41.6% of self-reported net income, and audit-threat letters moved only the self-reported part. Goodhart's law weakens when the datum comes from someone with no stake in it.

That predicts which disclosure works. Cardiac surgery report cards let surgeons pick their patients, and [Dranove and co-authors](https://www.nber.org/papers/w8697) found they "decreased patient and social welfare". The Toxics Release Inventory, where the figure is a physical release checkable against permits, coincided with [releases falling 54% over 25 years while GDP rose 75%](https://www.epa.gov/trinationalanalysis/introduction-2023-tri-national-analysis).

### Pay for the thing, not the volume

NHS England pays a fixed annual fee per antibiotic instead of per dose, in bands of £5m to £20m a product, a tender the [NAO](https://www.nao.org.uk/reports/investigation-into-how-government-is-addressing-antimicrobial-resistance/) values at almost £1.9 billion over 16 years. The design is right and largely unbuilt: as of mid-2026 only the two 2022 pilots run, and the UK is about 3% of the global antibiotic market. The US PASTEUR Act, the same idea, was introduced four times from 2020 and never left committee. See [health](../health/).

Advance market commitments buy supply reliably and invention unreliably. The $1.5 billion pneumococcal AMC had immunised over 215 million children by end-2019, but Gavi's own [evaluation](https://www.gavi.org/our-impact/evaluation-studies/pcv-amc-pilot-2nd-outcomes-impact) "did not find evidence that the PCV AMC pilot accelerated new product R&D": the target was [technologically close](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/pandp.20201017) when the money arrived.

### Binding a future government

This works when the constraint is attached to something visible. Norway limits structural spending to the expected 3% real return on a fund that [held NOK 22.7 trillion in mid-2026](https://www.nbim.no/en/investments/the-funds-value/), a ceiling tightened from 4% in 2017.

Future-generations offices are the weak version. Wales' 2015 Act creates a duty to follow the Commissioner's recommendations that a body escapes if it is [satisfied there is good reason, or decides on an alternative](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/anaw/2015/2/section/22) — both judged by the body itself. At ten years Audit Wales found the Act had raised prominence but was ["not driving the system-wide change that was intended"](https://www.audit.wales/publication/no-time-lose-lessons-our-work-under-well-being-future-generations-act). Hungary's 2008 ombudsman for future generations could suspend decisions and sue polluters; the 2012 successor may only propose. Offices with hard powers tend to lose them. See [politics](../politics/).

### What does not work

Producer responsibility funds collection but not redesign. The [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/modulated-fees-for-extended-producer-responsibility-schemes-epr_2a42f54b-en.html) finds "little evidence to suggest that collective EPR has instigated Design for Environment": collective schemes pool exactly the costs an individual redesign would save.

Better forecasts are not adopted when they are unwelcome. In the US intelligence community's own classified prediction market, forecasts beat the analytic reports they were drawn from ([Stastny and Lehner, 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/3360FC0D8835FC8B2C241F835C9288EB/S1930297500007105a.pdf): mean absolute error 0.302 against 0.416). Uptake stayed marginal anyway. An accurate forecast is a record someone can later be judged against, which is why organisations avoid keeping one — and why appeals to responsibility lose to whatever the payment scheme actually counts.
