## Problem

Antimicrobial resistance is rising while the antibiotic pipeline is nearly empty, because antibiotics are cheap and meant to be used rarely. Surgery, chemotherapy, intensive care and childbirth all assume working antibiotics.

Dense cities, industrial livestock and air travel make spillover of animal viruses more likely and much faster to spread. COVID-19 was mild compared to what is biologically possible.

Cheap gene synthesis and [AI](../ai/) design tools lower the skill needed to engineer a pathogen. Defence is slower than attack here.

Health systems must serve more old people with fewer workers. See [demographics](../demographics/).

Chronic disease from diet, inactivity and [pollution](../pollution/) already consumes most health spending.

## Research

### Resistance: two different death counts

The [GRAM study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39299261/) (Lancet, 2024) is the reference estimate. For 2021 it reports:

* 1.14 million deaths **attributable** to bacterial resistance — deaths that would not have happened if the infection had been drug-susceptible.
* 4.71 million deaths **associated** with it — deaths where a resistant infection was in the causal chain, though the patient might have died anyway.

The gap is a counterfactual choice, not a data error. Both are model outputs, and microbiology coverage is thinnest where the burden is highest.

From 1990 to 2021, attributable deaths in children under 5 fell 59.8% (488,000 to 193,000) as vaccination and child-survival programmes worked, while deaths over age 70 rose 89.7% to 519,000. Resistance is shifting to old age and invasive medicine. See [demographics](../demographics/).

Forecast for 2050: 1.91 million attributable and 8.22 million associated deaths per year, about 39 million cumulative attributable deaths over 2025-2050.

### Where the antibiotics go

Food animals consumed an estimated 99,502 tonnes of antimicrobials in 2020, projected to rise 8% by 2030 ([Mulchandani et al., 2023](https://journals.plos.org/globalpublichealth/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pgph.0001305)) — roughly 73% of world antimicrobial consumption in 2017, with China, Brazil, India, the USA and Australia at 58% of animal use. See [food](../food/).

### Why the pipeline is thin

WHO's [2025 pipeline review](https://www.who.int/news/item/02-10-2025-who-releases-new-reports-on-new-tests-and-treatments-in-development-for-bacterial-infections) counted 90 antibacterials in clinical development, down from 97 in 2023. Only 15 meet WHO's innovation criteria; only 5 act on a pathogen from WHO's "critical" list.

The economics invert: a new antibiotic that works should be held in reserve, so good stewardship destroys its revenue. Achaogen's plazomicin was approved in 2018, sold under US$1 million in its first year, and the firm went bankrupt in April 2019; the asset fetched about $16 million ([Kirchhelle et al., 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-024-03452-0)). NHS England's [subscription model](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/antimicrobial-products-subscription-model-guidance-on-commercial-arrangements/) instead pays a fixed annual fee per product, delinked from volume, about £100m/year from 2024/25. Whether that is large enough to restart private R&D is contested.

### Pandemic frequency is fat-tailed

[Marani et al. (PNAS, 2021)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2105482118) fitted epidemic intensity since 1600 to a generalized Pareto distribution with a slowly decaying power-law tail (asymptotic exponent -0.71). A fat tail means the historical average badly understates the risk of the largest events. Read their headline figures — about 0.3-1.9% per year for a 1918-scale event, ~38% lifetime chance of a COVID-scale one — as upper bounds: the authors [corrected the paper in 2023](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2302169120) after finding their code summed events across consecutive years, inflating the probabilities.

WHO put global excess deaths for January 2020 to December 2021 at [14.83 million](https://www.who.int/news/item/05-05-2022-14.9-million-excess-deaths-were-associated-with-the-covid-19-pandemic-in-2020-and-2021), against 5.42 million COVID-19 deaths reported to it — a factor of 2.74, with middle-income countries carrying 81% of the excess. Reported counts measure surveillance capacity as much as disease.

### Synthesis screening is voluntary

DNA synthesis costs have fallen by orders of magnitude since 2000; array-synthesised oligos are quoted at roughly $0.00001-$0.001 per base as of 2025 ([Carlson, 2025](http://www.synthesis.cc/synthesis/2025/5/dna-synthesis-and-sequencing-costs-and-productivity-for-2025)). Order screening is law nowhere. The International Gene Synthesis Consortium screens by member agreement; the US [2024 OSTP framework](https://www.aspr.gov/readiness-response/medical-countermeasures-biodefense/s3/synthetic-nucleic-acid-screening/ostp-framework-nucleic-acid-synthesis-screening) works through federal funding conditions rather than statute, and was ordered revised in 2025. Non-member providers and benchtop synthesisers sit outside it ([tracker](https://genesynthesisscreening.centerforhealthsecurity.org/key-policies)). Falling cost plus [AI](../ai/) design tools shifts the constraint from skill toward intent.

### Capacity and governance

WHO projects a shortfall of [11.1 million health workers by 2030](https://www.who.int/teams/health-workforce/about/2), revised up from 10.2 million, concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries and worsened by richer ones recruiting their staff. See [migration](../migration/).

The WHO Pandemic Agreement was adopted on 20 May 2025 but cannot open for signature until its Pathogen Access and Benefit Sharing annex is agreed. That deadline slipped; talks were [extended in May 2026](https://www.who.int/news/item/01-05-2026-who-member-states-agree-to-extend-negotiations-on-pathogen-access-and-benefit-sharing-annex), results are due by May 2027. See [coordination](../coordination/).
