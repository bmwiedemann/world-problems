## Problem

Water is renewable but locally finite. In many regions withdrawal already exceeds recharge.

Large aquifers are mined, not used: the Ogallala, the North China Plain, north-west India. They took millennia to fill and are being emptied within a lifetime. Some coastal ones take in seawater once drawn down and never recover.

Glaciers and snowpack act as the reservoir for the rivers of Asia and South America. They are shrinking, so flow shifts from a steady melt to flood-and-drought. See [climate change](../climate-change/).

About 70% of withdrawals go to irrigation, so a water shortage arrives as a [food](../food/) shortage.

Rivers and aquifers cross borders and belong to nobody. Upstream dams and pumping are already a source of conflict. See [politics](../politics/) and [war](../war/).

Desalination and long-distance transport work, but both trade water for [energy](../energy/).

## Research

### Where the water goes

* Global freshwater withdrawal was about 3,850 km3 in 2022, and has been roughly flat since 2015 ([World Bank / FAO AQUASTAT](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/ER.H2O.FWTL.K3)).
* The global split is 69% agriculture, 19% industry, 12% municipal ([FAO AQUASTAT](https://www.fao.org/aquastat/en/overview/methodology/water-use/)). Per-country averages give 59 / 18 / 23; the global figure is dominated by a few large irrigators.
* [WRI Aqueduct 4.0](https://www.wri.org/insights/highest-water-stressed-countries) (2023): 25 countries, a quarter of humanity, withdraw more than 80% of their renewable supply every year; about 4 billion people are over that line for at least one month a year. The indicator is withdrawal divided by available renewable supply, with 40% "high" and 80% "extremely high". It measures intensity of use, not shortage: a basin where withdrawals return to the river scores the same as one where they evaporate.

### Groundwater

GRACE (2002-2017) and GRACE-FO (2018-) weigh the continents from orbit at a footprint of a few hundred kilometres, and see *total* water storage. Groundwater is what remains after modelled soil moisture, snow, surface water and ice are subtracted, so the error is inherited from the land-surface model rather than the satellite, and small basins fall below the resolution.

* Of the 37 largest aquifer systems, 21 were losing storage over 2003-2013; nine depleting basins together exceeded 77 km3/yr ([JPL / Famiglietti 2015](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/study-third-of-big-groundwater-basins-in-distress/)). How much those aquifers still hold is largely unknown: the rate of loss is far better measured than the stock.
* North-west India: 17.7 +/- 4.5 km3/yr over 2002-2008, about 109 km3 lost ([Rodell et al. 2009](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature08238)). A [2016 reanalysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep24398) argues the signal-restoration method wrongly confined all depletion to three states and puts it at 14 +/- 0.4 km3/yr for 2005-2010, closer to the 2.8 cm/yr seen in well records. The gap is about a fifth of the signal and comes from method, not measurement.
* North China Plain: 8.3 +/- 1.1 km3/yr over 2003-2010, with deep-aquifer levels in parts of Hebei falling 1-3 m/yr ([Feng et al. 2013](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/wrcr.20192)); land subsidence follows.
* High Plains (Ogallala): recoverable storage in 2019 was roughly 286 million acre-feet, about 350 km3, below predevelopment ([USGS SIR 2023-5143](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/sir20235143/full)).
* Ground truth from 170,000 wells in 1,693 aquifer systems ([Jasechko et al. 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06879-8)): declines faster than 0.5 m/yr are widespread in dry cropland regions, and accelerated since the 1980s in 30% of aquifers - but reversed in 16%. Depletion is a policy outcome, not a physical inevitability.

### Ice

Glaciers lost 273 +/- 16 Gt/yr over 2000-2023, 36 +/- 10% faster in the second half than the first ([GlaMBIE, Nature 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08545-z)). Genuine glacier dependence is narrower than the headlines: seasonal meltwater covers the basic needs of about 221 +/- 59 million people, mostly in the upper Indus, Aral and Chu/Issyk-Kul basins, where melt dominates inflow in drought summers ([Pritchard 2019](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1240-1)). The Ganges and Brahmaputra are monsoon-fed; there glacier loss shifts timing more than annual volume.

### Desalination

Separating seawater at 35 g/L with 50% recovery has a thermodynamic minimum of 1.06 kWh/m3; good reverse-osmosis trains run near 2 kWh/m3, and whole plants, with intake, pretreatment and delivery, use more ([Joule 2024](https://www.cell.com/joule/fulltext/S2542-4351%2824%2900373-8)). The remaining headroom is a factor of about two, not ten - see [energy](../energy/). As of 2019, 15,906 plants produced 95 million m3/day of water and 141.5 million m3/day of brine; Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Kuwait and Qatar accounted for 55% of the brine ([Jones et al. 2019](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0048969718349167)).

### Shared water and virtual water

An estimated 313 rivers and lakes and 468 aquifers are shared between states, and 153 UN members depend on water crossing a border. As of 2024, 59% of transboundary basin area had an operational arrangement: a joint body, annual meetings, shared plans ([UN-Water, SDG 6.5.2](https://www.unwater.org/publications/progress-transboundary-water-cooperation-2024-update)).

Agriculture is 92% of humanity's 9,087 Gm3/yr water footprint (rain and pollution-dilution included, so larger than withdrawals) over 1996-2005, and about a fifth of it is embedded in exports ([Hoekstra & Mekonnen 2012](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1109936109)). Importing grain is importing water. That is how dry, rich countries keep eating, and how one basin's drought becomes another's price shock. See [food](../food/).
