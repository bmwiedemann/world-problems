## Problem

CO2 is a potent greenhouse gas and humans have been emitting increasing amounts of it since 1850. First burning coal, then oil and natural gas.

Methane CH4 and nitrous oxide N2O are even more potent, but not emitted in the same amounts, so their influence on the climate is not as significant as CO2's.

Deniers often point to other sources such as volcanic eruptions, variations in solar activity or natural cycles as explanations. However scientific models account for these and still conclude that man-made greenhouse gases are the significant driver behind rising global temperatures.

Warming is not the damage. The damage arrives as failed harvests, shifting water, dying ecosystems and people who move. See [food](../food/), [water](../water/), [biodiversity](../biodiversity/) and [migration](../migration/).

Emissions come from burning fuel, so this is largely an [energy](../energy/) problem.

Parts of the system may not return once pushed: ice sheets, permafrost, forests. Those changes are not reversible by cutting emissions later.

Everyone pays for their own reductions and shares the benefit with everyone else, which is why 30 years of conferences produced little. See [coordination](../coordination/).

## Research

[IPCC](https://www.ipcc.ch/) assessment reports are the standing synthesis; AR6 was completed in 2021-2023 and AR7 is in preparation. The numbers below come from the observational programmes that feed it.

### Warming so far

[Copernicus](https://climate.copernicus.eu/) puts 2025 at 1.47 °C above the 1850-1900 baseline, third-warmest after 2024 (1.60 °C) and 2023 (1.48 °C). The 2023-2025 three-year mean is the first multi-year period above 1.5 °C. AR6 assessed 1.09 °C for the decade 2011-2020, so the trend is roughly 0.2 °C per decade. Single years swing about 0.15 °C with El Nino and La Nina, which is why the Paris target is read as a 20-year mean, not a single year. [NASA GISS](https://data.giss.nasa.gov/gistemp/) and [NOAA](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/) maintain independent records that agree within a few hundredths of a degree.

### Greenhouse gases

* CO2: 425.7 ppm global mean in 2025, 52% above the pre-industrial ~278 ppm ([Global Carbon Project](https://globalcarbonbudget.org/)).
* Growth at Mauna Loa: 3.32 ppm in 2023, 3.33 ppm in 2024 (the largest on record), 2.23 ppm in 2025 ([NOAA GML](https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/)). The 2010s averaged roughly 2.4 ppm/yr.
* Radiative forcing in 2024 relative to 1750: CO2 2.33 W/m2, CH4 0.57, N2O 0.23. NOAA's [Annual Greenhouse Gas Index](https://gml.noaa.gov/aggi/aggi.html) stood at 1.54, a 54% rise in forcing since 1990.

Methane traps far more heat per molecule than CO2 but leaves faster: its atmospheric lifetime is about 12 years against 109 years for N2O, while a fraction of a CO2 pulse stays for millennia. AR6 gives 100-year global warming potentials of about 30 for fossil CH4 and 273 for N2O. Cutting methane cools within a couple of decades; cutting CO2 mostly stops further warming rather than undoing it.

### Emissions

Fossil CO2 reached a record 38.1 GtCO2 in 2025, up 1.1%, plus 4.1 GtCO2 from land-use change. China emitted about 32%, the United States 13%, India 8%. By sector, AR6 WG3 splits 2019 global greenhouse gases roughly into energy supply 34%, industry 24%, agriculture and land use 22%, transport 15%, buildings 6% - see [energy](../energy/) and [food](../food/).

### Budgets and sensitivity

The Global Carbon Project puts the remaining budget for a 50% chance of holding 1.5 °C at about 170 GtCO2, four years at 2025 rates. AR6 gave roughly 1150 GtCO2 from 2020 for 2 °C at the same odds. Equilibrium climate sensitivity - warming per doubling of CO2 - is assessed at 3 °C, likely 2.5-4 °C, very likely 2-5 °C. That range, narrowed in AR6 mainly by paleoclimate evidence, is the largest single uncertainty in long-run projections. Aerosol cooling and cloud feedbacks are the weakest links.

### Sea level and tipping elements

Satellites show about 10 cm of global mean rise since 1993, at roughly 4.4 mm/yr as of 2025 - more than double the early-1990s rate ([NASA](https://sealevel.nasa.gov/)). AR6 projects 0.28-0.55 m by 2100 under the lowest scenario and 0.63-1.01 m under the highest, with deep uncertainty above that from Antarctic ice-sheet instability.

The [Global Tipping Points](https://global-tipping-points.org/) report of 2025 judged warm-water coral reefs to have crossed their threshold already. Greenland and West Antarctic ice loss and permafrost thaw are gradual and hard to reverse rather than switch-like. AMOC weakening is genuinely contested: observational records are short and models disagree on whether a bistable collapse is reachable this century ([Carbon Brief explainer](https://www.carbonbrief.org/amoc-is-global-warming-tipping-key-atlantic-ocean-currents-towards-collapse)).
