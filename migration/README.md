## Problem

Heat, drought, sea-level rise and harvest failure will make parts of the tropics and subtropics much harder to inhabit. See [climate change](../climate-change/), [water](../water/) and [food](../food/).

Population growth is concentrated in Africa and South Asia, while ageing rich countries shrink. See [demographics](../demographics/).

The resulting gradient in wages and safety will move hundreds of millions of people. Not as one event, but as steady pressure over decades.

Rich countries need immigrants to sustain their workforce and pensions, and their voters increasingly reject them. Both things are true at once.

Receiving societies respond with backlash, which pulls [politics](../politics/) toward the short term exactly when long-term decisions are needed.

## Research

As of 2024 there were 304 million international migrants, people living outside their country of birth, up from 154 million in 1990. That is 3.7% of world population, against 2.9% in 1990 ([UN DESA, International Migrant Stock 2024](https://www.un.org/development/desa/pd/content/international-migrant-stock)). The absolute number nearly doubled while the share moved less than one point: migration has tracked population growth, not outrun it.

Also from that dataset, 2024:

* Hosts: Europe 94 million, Northern America 61 million, the United States alone 52.4 million.
* 45% of migrants lived inside their own region of origin; for those born in sub-Saharan Africa, 64%. Most movement is short-range, not South-to-North.
* 51.7 million, one in six, were refugees, asylum seekers or others needing international protection, the highest share since the series began in the 1950s.

UNHCR counted 117.8 million forcibly displaced at end-2025 (41.6 million refugees, 9 million asylum seekers, 68.7 million internally displaced), 5.9 million fewer than a year earlier and the first decline in a decade, driven by returns to Afghanistan, Syria and Sudan, many under poor conditions ([UNHCR Global Trends](https://www.unhcr.org/global-trends)). Most displacement never crosses a border: 62.2 million new internal displacements during 2025, 32.3 million from conflict and 29.9 million from disasters ([IDMC GRID 2026](https://www.internal-displacement.org/global-report/grid2026/)).

The World Bank's [Groundswell Part 2](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2021/09/13/climate-change-could-force-216-million-people-to-migrate-within-their-own-countries-by-2050) (2021) projects up to 216 million movers by 2050 across six regions: sub-Saharan Africa 86 million, East Asia and Pacific 49 million, South Asia 40 million, and 41 million across North Africa, Latin America, Eastern Europe and Central Asia. Two caveats usually get dropped. It models *internal* movement only, and only slow-onset drivers (water stress, crop yields, sea-level rise). And 216 million is the pessimistic end of a spread; the low-emissions, inclusive-development case is about 80% smaller. See [climate change](../climate-change/).

A wet-bulb temperature of 35 °C is the classic survivability threshold: above it sweat cannot shed metabolic heat, whatever the acclimatisation. [Raymond et al. 2020](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.aaw1838) found brief exceedances already recorded near the Persian Gulf, the Indus valley and the Gulf of California, and occurrences above 27 °C wet bulb more than doubling in frequency since 1979. Chamber experiments put the practical limit lower: uncompensable heat stress in young healthy adults at light activity began near 30.6 °C wet bulb in humid conditions ([Vecellio et al. 2022](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00737.2021)), lower again for the old and sick. Outdoor labour capacity fails long before a place becomes literally lethal.

The widely repeated "200 million climate refugees by 2050" traces to Norman Myers in the 1990s and 2000s. It counts people in exposed places rather than modelling who moves; Myers called the method a heroic extrapolation. Demographers treat it as an exposure estimate, not a projection ([review of the projections, 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2025.1570995/full); [critique, 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2590332222003761)). [IPCC AR6 WGII](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGII_SummaryForPolicymakers.pdf) states with high confidence that climate drives mobility, but puts no number on cross-border climate migration and notes responses are mediated by economic and political conditions. Migration costs money: the poorest affected often become less mobile, not more.

The test case for receiving labour markets is the 1980 Mariel boatlift, about 125,000 Cubans arriving in Miami within months. Card (1990) found no effect on native wages or unemployment. Borjas (2017) reanalysed it restricted to non-Hispanic male high-school dropouts aged 25-59 and found a large wage fall. Peri and Yasenov (2019) showed that restriction leaves 15-20 Miami observations per year, with plausible measurement error of 20-30%. The dispute is about sample construction, and unsettled ([summary](https://www.bruegel.org/blog-post/mariel-boatlift-controversy); [survey of refugee-wave studies](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/research-wage-and-employment-impact-refugees-shows-modest-or-no-harmful-effects)). The 2016 US National Academies review found the overall wage effect on native workers very small over 10 years or more, concentrated where negative on earlier immigrants and native high-school dropouts, with little evidence of employment effects ([NASEM 2016](https://www.nationalacademies.org/projects/DBASSE-CNSTAT-13-03/publication/23550)).
