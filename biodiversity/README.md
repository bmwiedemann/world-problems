## Problem

Wild animal and insect populations are shrinking. The causes are habitat loss, land-use change, pesticides, invasive species and warming, acting together.

Ecosystems supply pollination, soil formation, pest control, water filtration and fisheries at no cost. There is no affordable technical substitute at that scale. See [food](../food/).

The ocean absorbs a large share of our CO2 and acidifies in the process. This hits shell-forming plankton and corals at the base of marine food webs, which billions of people depend on for protein. See [climate change](../climate-change/).

Ecosystems tend to hold and then flip rather than degrade smoothly, so warnings come late.

Extinction is irreversible on human timescales. This is the one problem here where mistakes cannot be corrected later.

## Research

### How much has been lost

* The [Living Planet Index](https://www.worldwildlife.org/publications/2024-living-planet-report/) 2024 edition reports a 73% average decline in monitored vertebrate populations from 1970 to 2020, across about 35,000 populations of 5,495 species. Freshwater -85%, terrestrial -69%, marine -56%; Latin America and the Caribbean -95%.
* The [IUCN Red List](https://www.iucnredlist.org/), version 2026-1 (9 July 2026), holds 175,909 assessed species, of which 49,505 are threatened (Vulnerable, Endangered or Critically Endangered). That is about 28% of everything assessed. ([IUCN release](https://iucn.org/press-release/202607/desert-frog-deep-sea-molluscs-remarkable-species-risk-iucn-red-list))
* The [IPBES Global Assessment](https://www.ipbes.net/global-assessment) (2019) estimated around 1 million species threatened with extinction, 75% of the land surface significantly altered, 66% of the ocean under rising cumulative impact, and over 85% of wetland area lost since 1700.

### What those numbers do not say

The LPI is a geometric mean of population rates of change, not a headcount. A 73% decline does not mean 73% of animals are gone. [Leung et al. 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-020-2920-6) showed the global figure is driven by under 3% of the populations in the dataset; remove those extreme decliners and the remaining ~98.6% show no mean trend. ZSL's reply is that treating extreme increases symmetrically leaves the index roughly unchanged. A separate critique, [Buschke et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-03708-8), argues the index is sensitive to how populations near zero are handled. Coverage is also skewed to temperate vertebrates with long monitoring records; tropics and invertebrates are thin. [Our World in Data](https://ourworldindata.org/living-planet-index-decline) has a readable walkthrough of the arithmetic.

The Red List has the same problem in a different shape. 175,909 assessments cover roughly 2.1 million described species, out of an estimated 8 million or more eukaryotes. Birds, mammals and amphibians are near-completely assessed; insects, fungi and marine invertebrates are barely started. The 28% threatened share is not drawn from a random sample.

### Rate

Background extinction is estimated at roughly 0.1 to 2 extinctions per million species-years (E/MSY); [De Vos et al. 2015](https://conbio.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/cobi.12380) argue for the low end. [Ceballos et al. 2015](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.1400253), using a deliberately conservative 2 E/MSY baseline, put modern vertebrate losses up to 100 times higher. The planetary-boundaries framework ([Richardson et al. 2023](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adh2458), updated in the [Planetary Health Check](https://www.planetaryhealthcheck.org/)) sets the genetic-diversity boundary at under 10 E/MSY against a present rate above 100, and a functional-integrity boundary of under 10% human appropriation of net primary production against about 30% today. Six of nine boundaries are transgressed.

### Insects

The [Krefeld study](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0185809) (Hallmann et al. 2017) measured a 76% seasonal and 82% midsummer decline in flying insect biomass over 27 years, from Malaise traps in 63 German protected areas. It is biomass, not species counts, from one region, with no pre-1989 baseline. The broadest replication, [van Klink et al. 2020](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.aax9931) (166 surveys, 1,676 sites), found terrestrial insect abundance falling about 9% per decade while freshwater insects rose about 11% per decade, both driven largely by North America and Europe. That paper attracted an erratum and published Comments. The direction is agreed on; the magnitude and how far it generalises are not.

### Pollination and ocean chemistry

The [IPBES pollinators assessment](https://www.ipbes.net/assessment-reports/pollinators) (2016) found about 75% of leading global crop types depend at least partly on animal pollination, but only about 35% of production volume, because cereals and root crops are wind- or self-pollinated. So pollinator loss hits fruit, nuts and vegetables, and therefore nutrition, well before it hits calories. See [food](../food/).

Surface ocean pH has fallen from about 8.2 to 8.1 since preindustrial times, roughly 30% more hydrogen ions, per [NOAA](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/ocean-coasts/ocean-acidification). Dissolved CO2 forms carbonic acid, which releases H+; the extra H+ consumes carbonate ions, lowering the aragonite saturation state that corals, pteropods and coccolithophores need to build shells. The rate is the unusual part, not the value. See [climate change](../climate-change/).
